Updates are part of everyday maintenance

A PC that still works can still need urgent attention.

At home and at work, updates are easily postponed because a restart feels inconvenient or the computer appears to be running normally. Unresolved update failures can also go unnoticed. Working normally does not prove that a device is protected.

01

What happens when updates are missed?

Security updates repair weaknesses that attackers may exploit. Delaying them can leave a known weakness open for longer. Outdated software can also miss reliability fixes and become incompatible with newer applications or services.

For home users, a successful attack could expose private information or put access to accounts and photographs at risk. For businesses, disruption can stop staff working, delay customer service and create recovery costs. Missing an update does not guarantee an attack, but it can increase avoidable exposure.

Get help with failed updates ↗
02

Update more than Windows.

Browsers, office applications and device software also need attention. Routers and CCTV equipment may have manufacturer firmware updates. Check that the product is still supported and use trusted update sources.

Enable automatic updates where suitable, allow required restarts and check that installation completed. Businesses should assign responsibility, record supported versions and plan maintenance around essential work. Backups and compatibility checks help prepare for larger changes.

Cybersecurity awareness for homes and businesses

Recognise the risks before they disrupt your day.

Current cyber threats affect personal devices, cloud accounts and small organisations as well as large companies. Understanding common attack methods helps you choose practical safeguards and seek help sooner.

01

Phishing and payment scams

Unexpected messages can imitate a supplier, bank, delivery company or colleague. They may ask you to sign in on a fake page, open an attachment or approve an urgent payment. A familiar logo or sender name is not proof that a request is genuine.

Verify changes to payment details and unusual requests through a contact route you already trust. Businesses should make it easy for staff to report concerns promptly. People, technical controls and clear processes work together.

NCSC: defending against phishing ↗
02

Malware and ransomware

Malicious software can disrupt a device or access information. Ransomware can make files unavailable, and an incident may also involve data theft. Shared storage and connected systems can widen the disruption.

Keep supported software updated, limit unnecessary access and maintain separate, tested backups. If you suspect an infection, seek guidance before continuing to use affected equipment. Recovery depends on the incident and the condition of the available backups.

NCSC: reducing malware and ransomware risk ↗
03

Stolen accounts and reused passwords

A stolen password can let someone impersonate you, read email or misuse connected services. Reusing a password can put several accounts at risk if one service is compromised.

Use unique passwords, two-step verification where available and secure recovery arrangements. Review unexpected sign-in alerts through the genuine service. Do not approve a login prompt you did not initiate or share authentication codes with someone claiming to provide support.

Discuss an account concern ↗
04

Old equipment and unnecessary access

Support status matters for PCs, routers and cameras. Equipment can continue operating after its manufacturer stops providing security fixes. Default passwords, shared logins and unnecessary remote access can add further risk.

Review who needs access and remove it when no longer required. Ask about firmware support and secure settings before exposing equipment online. Where suitable, separate guest and security devices from business systems so access follows a clear purpose.

Technology checks that matter now

Keep your devices useful, supported and protected.

Reliable technology needs more than a quick fix. These practical checks help you plan upgrades, protect important accounts and avoid preventable disruption.

01

Still using Windows 10?

Standard Windows 10 support ended on 14 October 2025. Your computer can still work, but continued use needs a clear plan for security updates and supported software. Extended Security Updates are available for eligible devices under Microsoft's current programme.

Before replacing a working PC, check Windows 11 compatibility, the applications you rely on and how your files will be backed up and transferred. An operating-system upgrade and a hardware performance upgrade solve different problems.

Discuss your computer ↗Microsoft: current Windows 10 support guidance ↗
02

Plan a change around the work you need to keep doing.

Before an upgrade, list the applications, printers and other equipment you rely on. Check licences, account access and whether the software supports the proposed operating system. Prepare a backup before moving important information.

For a business, agree who will test essential tasks and when the change can take place. For a home user, consider access to email, documents and everyday devices. A clear plan makes it easier to check that the new setup meets the actual requirement.

Plan a setup or migration ↗
03

A backup only helps if you can restore it.

Identify the documents, photographs and business records you could not easily replace. Keep a separate backup, check that it completes and practise restoring a sample file before you depend on it.

Cloud synchronisation is convenient, but changes or deletions can also synchronise. Check version history, recovery options and retention rather than assuming a folder in the cloud is a complete recovery plan. Before repair or migration, discuss the condition of the storage and the safety of important files.

Discuss backup and recovery ↗
04

Good broadband does not guarantee good Wi-Fi.

A fast connection at the router can still leave meeting rooms, upstairs spaces or cameras with unreliable coverage. Walls, interference, access-point placement and the number of connected devices all affect the experience.

Describe where the problem happens and whether it affects one device or several. For an office move or CCTV installation, plan cabling, coverage, power and authorised access together. This makes it easier to choose suitable equipment and avoid adding devices to an already struggling network.

Make the first conversation useful

A few details help us plan the right support.

Tell us which device or system is affected, what you see on screen and when the problem began. Mention recent updates, new equipment or changes to your internet connection. A photograph of an error message can be useful, provided it does not expose private information.

Before a visit or remote session

  • Have the device make and model, your postcode and preferred contact time ready.
  • Explain whether the problem affects one device, a whole room or everyone at the property.
  • Mention any important files that are not backed up, unusual drive noises or suspected account compromise.
  • Keep relevant chargers and equipment available. Share no passwords, payment details or verification codes in your initial message.

For a new office or CCTV setup, describe the rooms, users, equipment and coverage you need. For an existing fault, explain what has already been tried so the next step builds on useful information.
